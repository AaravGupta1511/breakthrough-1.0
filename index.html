How it works

  1. Powered ascent

    The rocket flies in the plane that gives your chosen inclination, starting with the eastward push of Earth’s rotation. Each 0.1 s step integrates gravity, thrust (varying with air pressure), drag in an exponential atmosphere and propellant use, following the rocket equation Δv = Isp·g₀·ln(m₀/m₁). Guidance rises vertically, pitches over into a gravity turn, then steers closed-loop to orbit.

  2. Coasting and burns

    Once the engines stop, the spacecraft follows an exact two-body conic (universal-variable Kepler propagation), so time warp never adds error. Manoeuvres are impulsive burns: circularisation at apogee, GTO injection at an equator crossing, a Lambert-solved trans-lunar injection, and capture into lunar orbit after entering the Moon’s sphere of influence.

  3. To the planets

    A launch-window search solves Lambert’s problem around the Sun over the next synodic period to find the cheapest departure date. It picks Perseverance’s real July 2020 window when run for 2020. The rocket launches into a parking orbit aimed along the escape direction and burns onto a hyperbola. On leaving Earth’s sphere of influence the spacecraft switches to orbiting the Sun (patched conics) and makes a trajectory correction. At the planet it trims its approach and brakes into orbit. For moons, a second Lambert search plans the transfer from the capture orbit.

  4. The neighbourhood

    Satellites and debris move on Keplerian orbits with J2 nodal drift, which is why sun-synchronous orbits really stay aligned with the Sun. The Sun, Moon and planets come from standard low-precision ephemerides, which drive the lighting, the day/night terminator, eclipses and every distance read-out.

Accuracy & data

Educational, not operational. Rocket figures are approximations of published values, and the ascent uses simplified aerodynamics and guidance. Burns after orbit insertion are instantaneous.

Satellites use representative orbits: correct altitudes, inclinations and shapes, and real longitudes for geostationary spacecraft, but not live tracking data, so positions along each orbit are illustrative.

Debris is generated to match the real population’s altitude and inclination structure. The count is adjustable; tens of thousands of objects are tracked in reality.

Moons of other planets move on circular orbits in their planet’s equatorial plane with the right sizes and periods; where they are along those orbits is representative. Missions fly direct transfers without gravity assists, so some burns are larger than real missions need.

Ephemerides for the Sun, Moon and planets are accurate to a fraction of a degree between 1800 and 2050. Coastlines are from Natural Earth (public domain). 3D rendering uses three.js.
